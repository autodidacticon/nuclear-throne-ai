# Phase 9: Computer Vision Pipeline for Extracting RL Training Data from Gameplay Videos

## Part 1: Feasibility Report

### Overall Verdict: NO -- not feasible as a drop-in replacement for structured logging

Extracting observation vectors from video that are accurate enough to substitute for the current NTT recording mod is not practically feasible. However, a **hybrid approach** using video-derived data with a degraded observation space could produce a policy meaningfully better than random, given sufficient engineering investment (estimated 3-6 months). The recommendation is to continue using the NTT mod for high-quality data and treat video extraction as a secondary, experimental data source.

### Per-Field Feasibility Assessment

#### 1. Player Position -- MEDIUM

The player character is always near the center of the screen in Nuclear Throne (camera follows the player). However, the player is NOT always exactly centered -- the camera has smoothing/lag and shifts toward the crosshair. The player's position relative to the room is what matters for the observation vector, and this requires knowing the camera offset, which is not directly visible.

- **Detection**: The player sprite is distinctive and consistent. Template matching against known character sprites (Fish, Crystal, etc.) would work well at native resolution. At YouTube 1080p with pixel-perfect 4x scaling, subpixel accuracy is achievable.
- **Position in room coordinates**: This is the hard part. The observation needs `x / 10080.0` (room coordinates), not screen coordinates. You would need to track the cumulative camera position across frames, which drifts with error. Level geometry could provide anchor points, but rooms are procedurally generated.
- **Screen shake**: Nuclear Throne has extreme screen shake on weapon fire. This shifts the entire rendered frame by random offsets each frame. Without compensating for shake, position estimates jitter by up to 10-20 pixels per frame.
- **Accuracy**: Screen-relative position is HIGH feasibility. Room-absolute position is LOW feasibility -- cumulative drift would make positions meaningless within 5-10 seconds.

#### 2. Player HP -- HIGH

HP is displayed as a UI element in the top-left corner. It renders as a row of small icons (full/half/empty).

- Template matching or a small trained classifier on the HP region would reliably extract current HP.
- Max HP changes when leveling up or picking up certain mutations, but the UI reflects this.
- **Accuracy**: 95%+ achievable. UI elements are rendered at fixed screen positions, resistant to compression artifacts.

#### 3. Enemy Positions -- MEDIUM

Enemies are distinct sprites that move on screen. The challenge is detection in a visually noisy environment.

- Nuclear Throne has dense visual effects: explosions, bullet impacts, blood splatter, screen shake, projectile trails. These occlude enemies frequently.
- Enemies overlap each other in crowded situations.
- At 320x240 native resolution, most enemies are 8-24 pixels wide. After YouTube compression to 1080p, these become 32-96 pixels with compression artifacts, but pixel art upscaling is relatively clean.
- A YOLO-style detector trained on Nuclear Throne sprites could achieve reasonable detection rates, but would require:
  - Manually annotated training data (bounding boxes on 5,000-10,000 frames)
  - OR synthetic training data generated by compositing known sprites onto level backgrounds
- Template matching is fragile because enemies have multiple animation frames, rotations, and are partially occluded.
- **Position accuracy**: Screen-relative positions are feasible. Converting to room coordinates has the same drift problem as player position.
- **Count accuracy**: Expect 60-80% detection rate. Enemies behind walls, partially offscreen, or obscured by effects will be missed. False positives from corpses, props, and effects.

#### 4. Enemy Types -- LOW-MEDIUM

- Different enemy types have distinct sprites (Bandits, Maggots, Scorpions, etc.), but:
  - Many share similar silhouettes at low resolution
  - Animation states change appearance significantly
  - Elite/champion variants look similar to base enemies
  - The observation uses `hitid` (an integer type identifier), which would need a learned mapping from visual appearance
- A classifier on detected enemy crops could distinguish major enemy categories (melee vs ranged, small vs large) but not specific subtypes reliably.
- **Accuracy**: 40-60% for broad categories, 20-30% for specific type IDs.

#### 5. Enemy HP -- LOW (effectively impossible)

Nuclear Throne does NOT display enemy health bars. There is no visual indicator of how much HP an enemy has remaining, except:
- Some enemies change appearance when heavily damaged (rare)
- You can count how many hits you've landed (impractical at video framerate)

This field would have to be set to a constant (e.g., 1.0 = assume full health) or estimated from how long the enemy has been visible while being shot. Neither produces useful signal.

#### 6. Projectile Positions and Velocities -- LOW

This is one of the hardest fields to extract:

- Enemy projectiles are very small (2-6 pixels at native resolution)
- They move fast (8-16 pixels per frame at 30 FPS)
- They overlap with player projectiles, explosions, and particle effects
- YouTube compression at 30fps means fast projectiles may only appear in 1-3 frames between spawn and impact
- Velocity estimation requires tracking the same projectile across frames, which is extremely difficult with small, similar-looking sprites moving in a sea of visual noise
- Damage and lifetime values are internal game state with zero visual representation
- **Accuracy**: Position detection maybe 30-40%. Velocity estimation maybe 20-30%. Damage and lifetime are impossible from video.

#### 7. Wall Positions / Raycasts -- MEDIUM-HIGH

Level geometry is clearly visible as walls are visually distinct from floor tiles.

- Semantic segmentation of "wall vs floor" is feasible with a trained model or even color/texture heuristics (walls are darker, have distinct tile patterns per area)
- Computing raycast distances (distance to nearest wall in 4 cardinal directions from player position) requires knowing the player's position first, then scanning the segmentation mask
- The segmentation would need to be area-specific (Desert, Sewers, Scrapyard all have different visual styles)
- **Accuracy**: 70-85% for wall distance estimates, assuming good player position detection.

#### 8. Human Actions -- MEDIUM-HIGH (partial)

- **Movement direction**: Can be inferred from player position delta between frames. At 30fps with ~3 pixel/frame movement speed, this is noisy but feasible for 8-directional discretization. Screen shake corrupts this unless compensated. **Accuracy: 70-80%** after shake compensation.
- **Aim angle**: The crosshair/cursor position is visible on screen as a distinct sprite. Detecting it and computing the angle from player to crosshair gives aim direction. The crosshair is small but has a unique appearance. **Accuracy: 80-90%** if crosshair detection works.
- **Fire**: Detectable from muzzle flash, bullet spawn near player, or weapon animation. Muzzle flashes are bright and distinctive. **Accuracy: 70-80%**.
- **Special ability**: Character-specific animations (Crystal shield, Fish roll, etc.). Would require per-character classifiers. **Accuracy: 50-60%**.

#### 9. Game State -- HIGH

- **Area/level**: Displayed as UI text, and also visually obvious from the tileset. Level transition screens show the area name explicitly. **Accuracy: 95%+**.
- **Kill count**: Displayed as a UI counter. OCR on a fixed screen region. **Accuracy: 95%+**.
- **Enemies remaining**: Not displayed on screen. Would have to be estimated from detected enemy count, which is unreliable.

### Why the Overall Verdict is NO

The 240-float observation vector was designed around complete game state access. Several critical fields are either invisible or extremely difficult to extract from video:

1. **Enemy HP** (20 enemies x 2 fields = 40 floats): No health bars displayed. This is essential for combat decisions (focus fire vs. spread damage, threat prioritization).

2. **Projectile state** (20 projectiles x 6 fields = 120 floats): Half the observation vector is projectile data. Projectile detection from video is extremely unreliable. Damage and lifetime are invisible. This is the primary "dodge" signal for a bullet-hell game.

3. **Room-absolute coordinates**: The observation normalizes all positions by room dimensions (10,080 pixels). Without a reliable camera-to-room transform, all spatial features are wrong. You could use screen-relative coordinates instead, but this changes the observation semantics fundamentally.

4. **Accuracy compounds badly**: Even if each field is 70% accurate, with 240 fields the probability that any given observation vector is fully correct is vanishingly small. BC training is sensitive to observation noise because it learns a direct mapping from observation to action.

The current BC pipeline achieves 70% action prediction accuracy with perfect observations from 230K transitions. A CV pipeline producing 40-60% accurate observations would likely need 10x more data to reach the same policy quality, and even then the noise floor might prevent convergence.

### What Partial Extraction Could Be Useful For

Even though full observation extraction is infeasible, video data could be valuable in a **reduced observation space**:

#### Option A: Screen-Relative Visual Features Only (Recommended if pursuing this)

Instead of extracting the 240-float structured vector, train a separate policy on a **pixel-based observation space**:

- Input: Downscaled game frame (e.g., 80x80 grayscale or 3-channel)
- Use a CNN policy (Nature DQN architecture or similar)
- Extract only the action labels from video (movement, aim, shoot)
- This sidesteps the entire observation extraction problem
- Disadvantage: requires a CNN policy architecture instead of the current MLP, and the two policies would not be interchangeable

This is essentially the approach used by DeepMind's Atari agents and would work for Nuclear Throne. The game's low native resolution (320x240) is actually advantageous here.

#### Option B: Hybrid Approach

Use video extraction for the fields that work well, and fill in the rest with defaults:

- Extract: player HP, aim angle, movement direction, area/level, shoot/fire, wall distances
- Default: enemy HP (assume 1.0), projectile fields (all zeros), room-absolute positions (use screen-relative instead)
- This gives a degraded but possibly useful signal for BC pre-training
- The BC policy would learn "shoot at visible things and avoid damage" without fine-grained threat assessment

#### Option C: Action-Only Extraction

The easiest and most impactful use of video data:

- Detect ONLY the human actions (movement direction, aim angle, fire, special)
- Pair these with whatever observations are available at inference time (from the game bridge)
- This is useful for **DAgger** (Dataset Aggregation) or reward shaping, not standard BC

## Part 2: Implementation Plan (Conditional -- for Option A, the most viable path)

Since the full structured observation extraction is not feasible, this plan covers **Option A: pixel-based BC from gameplay videos**. This requires rearchitecting the policy network but provides the most data leverage.

### Architecture

```
Video File (.mp4)
    |
    v
Frame Extractor (ffmpeg)
    |
    v
Per-Frame Pipeline:
    1. Crop to game viewport (remove streamer overlay, webcam, chat)
    2. Downscale to 80x80 (or 84x84 for Atari compatibility)
    3. Convert to grayscale (optional, reduces input size)
    4. Stack 4 consecutive frames (velocity/direction signal)
    |
    v
Action Extractor (per-frame):
    a. Movement direction: optical flow on player region
    b. Aim angle: crosshair detector + angle computation
    c. Fire: muzzle flash detector or projectile spawn detector
    d. Special: character animation classifier
    |
    v
Training Data: (frame_stack, action) pairs
    |
    v
CNN Policy (Nature DQN backbone + action heads)
    |
    v
BC Training (cross-entropy loss per action dimension)
```

### Pipeline Components

#### 1. Video Ingestion and Game Viewport Detection

**Goal**: Extract clean game frames from arbitrary video sources.

Nuclear Throne gameplay videos come in several formats:
- Clean recordings (just the game window) -- trivial
- YouTube/Twitch with webcam overlay -- need to detect and crop the game region
- Different resolutions (720p, 1080p, 1440p, 4K)
- Different aspect ratios (the game is 4:3 native but often pillarboxed in 16:9)

**Approach**:
1. Use ffmpeg to extract frames at 15fps (half of 30fps, sufficient for BC)
2. Detect the game viewport using the HUD elements as anchors (the HP bar and ammo counter are always in fixed positions relative to the game viewport)
3. Template-match the HUD corners to find the viewport rectangle
4. Crop and normalize to a fixed size

**Estimated accuracy**: 98%+ for viewport detection once calibrated per video source.

#### 2. Frame Preprocessing

```python
# Pseudocode
def preprocess_frame(raw_frame, viewport_rect):
    game_frame = crop(raw_frame, viewport_rect)
    game_frame = resize(game_frame, (80, 80), interpolation=NEAREST)  # preserve pixel art
    gray = cvtColor(game_frame, COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
    return gray  # uint8, shape (80, 80)
```

Key decision: use NEAREST-NEIGHBOR interpolation when downscaling to preserve the pixel art aesthetic and avoid blurring sprite edges into noise.

Frame stacking: maintain a deque of 4 frames, output shape `(4, 80, 80)`.

#### 3. Action Extraction

This is the most critical and difficult component.

##### 3a. Movement Direction

**Approach**: Compute optical flow in a small region around the player character. The dominant flow direction indicates movement.

```
Steps:
1. Detect player position in frame N and N+1 (template matching)
2. Compute displacement vector
3. Compensate for screen shake (estimate global frame shift from background)
4. Quantize to 8 directions + "none" based on displacement magnitude and angle
```

**Screen shake compensation**: Compute the median displacement of background tiles between frames. Subtract this global shift from the player displacement. This isolates true player movement from camera shake.

**Training data needed**: None (rule-based).
**Expected accuracy**: 70-80% for 9-class movement direction.

##### 3b. Aim Angle

**Approach**: Detect the crosshair sprite using template matching or a small classifier.

The Nuclear Throne crosshair is a small (8x8 or similar) white/colored sprite. It is unique in appearance and always present during gameplay.

```
Steps:
1. Search for crosshair sprite in the frame (template matching with multiple scales)
2. Compute angle from player center to crosshair center
3. Quantize to 24 bins (15-degree increments)
```

**Complication**: Some players use custom crosshairs (mods). For YouTube data, the crosshair might not be the default sprite. However, most NT videos use the default.

**Training data needed**: None (template matching). If custom crosshairs are common, train a small crosshair detector on ~500 annotated frames.
**Expected accuracy**: 80-90% on videos with default crosshair. 50-60% with custom crosshairs.

##### 3c. Fire Detection

**Approach**: Multi-signal detection:
1. **Muzzle flash**: Bright pixels near the weapon tip that appear for 1-2 frames
2. **Player projectile spawn**: A new moving object appears near the player
3. **Weapon animation**: The weapon sprite changes during firing

The most reliable signal is the muzzle flash, which is a bright, short-lived visual event.

```
Steps:
1. Define a region of interest around the player (24x24 pixel region)
2. Compute the brightness delta between frames in this region
3. If delta exceeds threshold, classify as "firing"
4. Additionally check for the spawn of projectile-like objects (small bright sprites moving away from player)
```

**Training data needed**: 1,000-2,000 annotated frames (firing/not-firing binary labels).
**Expected accuracy**: 70-80%.

##### 3d. Special Ability Detection

**Approach**: Character-specific animation classifier.

Each character has a unique special ability with a distinctive animation:
- Fish: dodge roll (character sprite changes, moves fast)
- Crystal: shield (crystal barrier appears around player)
- Eyes: telekinesis (enemies pulled toward player)
- Melting: explode corpses (corpses flash and disappear)
- etc.

This requires a per-character classifier, which is complex but each individual classifier is simple (binary: using special or not).

**Training data needed**: 500+ annotated frames per character.
**Expected accuracy**: 50-70% (highly character-dependent).

#### 4. CNN Policy Architecture

```python
import torch.nn as nn

class NTPixelPolicy(nn.Module):
    """Nature DQN-style backbone with multi-head action output."""

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        # Input: (batch, 4, 80, 80) -- 4 stacked grayscale frames
        self.features = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(4, 32, kernel_size=8, stride=4),   # -> (32, 19, 19)
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=4, stride=2),  # -> (64, 8, 8)
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Conv2d(64, 64, kernel_size=3, stride=1),  # -> (64, 6, 6)
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Flatten(),                                  # -> 2304
            nn.Linear(2304, 512),
            nn.ReLU(),
        )

        # Action heads (same MultiDiscrete space as structured pipeline)
        self.move_head = nn.Linear(512, 9)      # 8 directions + none
        self.aim_head = nn.Linear(512, 24)       # 24 angle bins
        self.shoot_head = nn.Linear(512, 2)      # binary
        self.special_head = nn.Linear(512, 2)    # binary

    def forward(self, x):
        features = self.features(x / 255.0)  # normalize pixels
        return {
            'move': self.move_head(features),
            'aim': self.aim_head(features),
            'shoot': self.shoot_head(features),
            'special': self.special_head(features),
        }
```

This architecture is well-understood (Mnih et al., 2015) and works on pixel-art games. The 80x80 input is slightly smaller than Atari's 84x84 but sufficient for NT's native 320x240 resolution.

#### 5. Training Pipeline

```python
# Pseudocode for training loop
dataset = VideoFrameDataset(
    frame_dirs=["processed/video_001/", "processed/video_002/", ...],
    action_labels="processed/actions.csv",
    frame_stack=4,
)

model = NTPixelPolicy()
optimizer = Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-4)

for epoch in range(50):
    for frames, actions in DataLoader(dataset, batch_size=64, shuffle=True):
        logits = model(frames)

        loss = (
            cross_entropy(logits['move'], actions[:, 0]) +
            cross_entropy(logits['aim'], actions[:, 1]) +
            cross_entropy(logits['shoot'], actions[:, 2]) +
            cross_entropy(logits['special'], actions[:, 3])
        )

        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()
```

**Class balancing**: Same approach as the existing BC pipeline -- per-dimension weighted cross-entropy to prevent action collapse (the "always move right and never shoot" failure mode).

#### 6. Training Data Requirements for CV Models

| Component | Annotated Frames Needed | Annotation Type | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viewport detection | 50-100 | Bounding box on game area | 1 hour |
| Player detection | 200-500 | Bounding box on player | 2-4 hours |
| Crosshair detection | 200-500 | Point annotation | 2-3 hours |
| Fire detection | 1,000-2,000 | Binary label per frame | 4-8 hours |
| Special detection | 500/character | Binary label per frame | 2-4 hours/char |
| Enemy detection (if needed) | 5,000-10,000 | Bounding boxes | 40-80 hours |

**Alternative**: Generate synthetic training data by compositing known game sprites onto random level backgrounds. This requires extracted sprite assets (which this project already has via the `npm run regen` pipeline). This could reduce manual annotation to near zero for player, crosshair, and enemy detection.

#### 7. Compute Requirements

- **Frame extraction**: ~1 hour of video produces ~54,000 frames at 15fps. 1TB of YouTube NT gameplay = ~500 hours = ~27M frames. Storage: ~200GB for 80x80 grayscale frames.
- **Action extraction**: CPU-bound, ~100 frames/sec with template matching. 27M frames = ~75 hours of processing.
- **CNN training**: The model is small (< 5M parameters). Training on 1M frame-action pairs takes ~2 hours on an M4 Max GPU (MPS) or ~30 minutes on an A100.
- **Total pipeline**: For 100 hours of video, expect ~2 days of processing end-to-end on an M4 Max.

#### 8. Expected Accuracy

**Action prediction accuracy targets** (for the pixel-based CNN policy):

| Action Dim | Baseline (random) | Target (CV-BC) | Current (structured BC) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Movement (9 classes) | 11% | 40-55% | ~70% |
| Aim (24 classes) | 4% | 25-40% | ~70% |
| Shoot (binary) | 50% | 70-80% | ~70% |
| Special (binary) | 50% | 60-70% | ~70% |

The pixel-based policy will be significantly worse than the structured-observation policy because:
1. Action labels extracted from video are noisy (see per-field accuracy above)
2. The CNN must learn spatial relationships from raw pixels that the structured policy gets for free
3. Video compression destroys fine details that matter for aim precision

However, even 40% movement accuracy and 70% shoot accuracy produces a policy that is clearly better than random: it moves toward enemies and shoots at them, which is the core gameplay loop.

### Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Screen shake makes action extraction unreliable | High | Global motion compensation (background optical flow subtraction) |
| YouTube compression destroys small sprites | High | Prioritize high-quality source videos; filter by resolution |
| Crosshair detection fails on non-default crosshairs | Medium | Fall back to gun angle estimation from weapon sprite orientation |
| Action label noise prevents BC convergence | High | Use confidence filtering (discard frames where action extraction confidence is low) |
| Different game versions have different sprites | Low | NT hasn't been updated since 2017; sprites are stable |
| Streamer overlays occlude game area | Medium | Filter videos to clean recordings only; community recordings are often clean |

### Recommendation

**Do not pursue video-based data extraction as a primary training data source.** The NTT recording mod produces perfect structured data at zero annotation cost. The engineering investment for a CV pipeline (7-12 weeks) would be better spent on:

1. Recording more demonstrations with the NTT mod (diminishing returns after ~500K transitions, but the current 230K could benefit from 2-3x more)
2. Improving the PPO fine-tuning loop (Phase 6+)
3. Adding weapon observability (Phase 7) and mutation selection (Phase 8)

**Consider video-based training only if**:
- You want to train on gameplay from expert players whose NTT recordings are unavailable
- You want to experiment with pixel-based policies (CNN/ViT) as a research direction
- You want to leverage the thousands of hours of NT footage on YouTube as a scaling experiment

If you do pursue it, start with **Option A (pixel-based BC)** rather than trying to reconstruct the 240-float structured observation from video. The pixel-based approach avoids the hardest CV problems (room-absolute coordinates, enemy HP, projectile internals) and has established precedent in the Atari RL literature.
